Abstract

Blood cell classifiers usually throw away two things the clinical problem gives them for free: the cell types are not unrelated labels but branches of a developmental tree, and the rare types that matter most diagnostically are the ones a normally trained model learns worst.

This dissertation asks whether making a classifier lineage-aware, and training it under an imbalance-robust objective, improves fine-grained classification of rare blood cell types against a flat transfer-learning baseline. Using MLL23 — 41,621 expert-annotated images across 18 classes at an imbalance ratio of 261:1 — a shared pretrained backbone feeds a lineage head and a cell-type head, coupled by a class-balanced hierarchical loss. Four configurations were trained under identical partitions and backbones at three seeds each and compared by paired testing with reported effect sizes.

The hierarchical model reached a test macro F1 of 0.879 against 0.874 for the baseline, and a minority-class macro F1 of 0.840 against 0.823 — improving the rare classes by more than the overall average, which is the predicted direction. Aggregate differences were not significant at three seeds, but the model significantly reduced cross-lineage error, the clinically severe category. Removing the imbalance term cost 0.060 minority macro F1, an order of magnitude more than the hierarchy contributed.

The problem

Accuracy hides exactly the failure that matters

A model trained on this corpus can abandon its rarest class completely and lose under 0.1% of accuracy. That is not a hypothetical. In this study the arm that scored highest on accuracy (0.9602) scored lowest on minority-class F1 (0.7799) — it bought its headline number by giving up on the tail.

That is why macro F1 and balanced accuracy are the headline metrics here, and why every result below is reported for the rare classes separately.

Method

One backbone, two heads, one coupled objective

A pretrained ViT-Small/16 backbone produces a pooled representation that feeds two linear heads — one predicting lineage (3 classes), one predicting cell type (18 classes). They train jointly, so the coarse decision shapes the shared representation and rare types can borrow strength from their lineage neighbours.

The lineage head is a regulariser, not a gate. A hard gate that routed each cell to a per-lineage sub-classifier would assume the lineages form separable clusters. They do not: on frozen features the lineage silhouette coefficient is approximately zero, while nearest-neighbour lineage agreement is 0.78 against a 0.45 chance baseline. The structure is real but local, so the coupling is soft.

Stripping out the class-balanced and focal terms costs 0.076 minority recall (p = 0.024) and 0.060 minority macro F1 (p = 0.052) — roughly ten times what the hierarchy contributes. On this evidence the imbalance-robust objective, not the architecture, is the load-bearing component.

Where the hierarchy actually helps

The myeloid maturation stages show no measurable benefit (mean difference −0.005 against a seed standard deviation of 0.011). Their confusable neighbours share their lineage label, so lineage supervision cannot separate them even in principle.

Principal finding

Errors travel one step along the maturation chain

Neutrophil development is an ordered sequence, and the labels cut it into six discrete classes:

myeloblast→promyelocyte→myelocyte→metamyelocyte→band→segmented

Pooling all three seeds gives 18,732 test predictions and 811 errors, 305 of them falling between two classes inside that chain. Scoring each by how many developmental stages separate truth from prediction produces an unusually clean answer.

Over a third of all errors are the mildest kind available, and the training objective charges each of them exactly what it charges for calling a lymphocyte a myeloblast. Nothing in the loss, and nothing in macro F1, knows these six classes lie in an order — which is the clearest opening the results leave for further work.

Myeloblasts are the exception that confirms the reading: their errors leave the chain entirely, going to typical lymphocytes (47%) and monocytes (22%). Telling blasts from lymphocytes is a separate and clinically graver problem that ordinal reasoning would not touch.

Interpretability

The model looks at the cell, not the background

Producing these on a Vision Transformer takes care. The classifier reads only the class token, so patch-token gradients at the final block are exactly zero and naive Grad-CAM returns a blank map that still looks like a real figure. Saliency is computed at the input to the final block's attention instead.
